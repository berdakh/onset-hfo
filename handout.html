Onset-HFO — the signal behind every number

High-frequency oscillations and interictal epileptiform discharges in public intracranial EEG, with an evidence-only assistant on an open-weight model.

We can show you the signal window behind every number this system reports — and the two times we were wrong.

What it does

  • Two HFO detectors differing in one thing — the feature they threshold (RMS energy, Staba 2002; line length, Gardner 2007) — so a disagreement is attributable to the feature, not to two implementations drifting apart.
  • Every event is checked against the recording's own 1/f background, so filter ringing — what a sharp transient becomes after an 80–250 Hz filter — is rejected rather than counted.
  • Rates carry Poisson intervals. Channels that cannot be separated from the leader are reported as a candidate set, not ranked into a false order.
  • A report with no recommendation field. Not empty — absent.

The direction Fedele et al. (2017) predicts. Thirteen against seven can only detect AUC ≥ 0.85 at 80% power, so this cohort could not have established either number. Of 36 comparisons in the full study, one reaches p = 0.034 uncorrected — fewer than the ~2 that 36 tests produce by chance, and its Bonferroni p is 1.00.

The most useful result. On the first 60 seconds of the same recordings — same code, same pre-specified metric — the expert arm gave AUC 0.82, p = 0.007, and that is what this project claimed until the study was re-run on the whole recording. Two patients account for the difference. A conclusion that changes between minute one and minutes one-to-five is not yet a measurement. Both tables are published side by side.

What it will not do

  • Ask the assistant which tissue to resect and it refuses — before any model runs, in code, not in a prompt.
  • No diagnosis, no treatment recommendation, no clinical validation. The randomised HFO Trial (2022) came out against HFO-guided tailoring; this project measures, it does not settle that.
